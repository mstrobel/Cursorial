tokyo-night — dropdown vs split disambiguation
Problem: a dropdown button (one target — whole thing opens a menu) and a split button (two targets — label acts, arrow opens a menu) both end in ▾. The split currently needs a │ separator cell to read as two zones. Below: six ways to encode the difference without spending a separator cell — mostly by changing the dropdown glyph or its cell treatment.

Each row shows the same two controls — a one-target dropdown and a two-target split — under a different encoding. The split's job is to look like “label + a separately-clickable arrow” without spending a │ cell. Single-channel cues (shape alone, colour alone) are weak at one-cell scale; the structural cue of giving the arrow its own tinted cell (B) is the clearest, and stacking it with an accent arrow (F) adds redundancy.

Disambiguation has to survive interaction. With the tinted-zone approach the two hit targets are obvious on hover: hovering the label lights only the action zone while the arrow keeps its standing tint; hovering the arrow inverts just that cell to accent. A plain dropdown button, by contrast, highlights as a single block — which is exactly the signal that it’s one target. No separator cell anywhere; the arrow’s background tint does all the structural work, and its accent colour reinforces “independently clickable.”

▸ At toolbar scale — mixed row

PasteCopy▾│Align▾Color▾Insert▾
In a real row the difference is immediate: Copy and Color (split) carry a tinted arrow cell, while Align and Insert (dropdown) have a bare muted caret hugging the label. A reader scans “does the arrow sit in its own shaded cell?” to know whether the label is independently clickable — no separator, one glyph cell each. Group separators (the real │) can still appear between unrelated clusters, but they’re no longer load-bearing for split-vs-dropdown.
